🐶 Year 7 Maths

Build the foundations for everything that comes next.

1. Place value and ordering numbers

Our number system is based on groups of ten. A digit's position determines its value. In 4,582, the 4 represents 4,000, the 5 represents 500, the 8 represents 80 and the 2 represents 2. Decimals extend the same idea: tenths, hundredths, then thousandths.

To order numbers, compare them from left to right. For decimals, line up decimal points and add zeroes at the end if it helps: 0.7 = 0.70.

Worked example: order 3.08, 3.8 and 3.18.
  1. Write them as 3.08, 3.80 and 3.18.
  2. The ones digits match. Compare tenths: 0, 8, 1.
  3. Smallest to largest: 3.08, 3.18, 3.8.
A zero at the end of a decimal does not change its value: 2.5 = 2.50.
2. Four operations and BIDMAS

Addition combines amounts; subtraction finds a difference; multiplication combines equal groups; division shares equally or counts groups. Estimate before calculating to check whether an answer is sensible.

Use BIDMAS for mixed calculations: Brackets, Indices, Division and Multiplication, Addition and Subtraction. Division and multiplication have equal priority, working left to right. Addition and subtraction also have equal priority.

Worked example: 6 + 3 × (8 − 5)²
  1. Brackets: 8 − 5 = 3.
  2. Indices: 3² = 9.
  3. Multiplication: 3 × 9 = 27.
  4. Addition: 6 + 27 = 33.
Do not automatically calculate from left to right when operations have different priorities.
3. Negative numbers

Negative numbers are below zero, like temperatures below freezing. On a number line, moving right makes numbers larger, so −2 is greater than −7.

Adding a negative moves left; subtracting a negative is the same as adding a positive. For multiplication and division, matching signs give a positive result; different signs give a negative result.

Worked example: −5 + 8 − 6

Start at −5, move 8 right to reach 3, then move 6 left to reach −3.

4. Factors, multiples, primes and powers

A factor divides exactly into a number. A multiple is made by multiplying a number by an integer. A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. The number 1 is not prime. Square numbers are n × n; cube numbers are n × n × n.

Worked example: prime factorise 60.

60 = 2 × 30 = 2 × 2 × 15 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5. So 60 = 2² × 3 × 5.

Prime factorisation helps find highest common factors (HCFs) and lowest common multiples (LCMs).

5. Fractions: meaning, equivalence and simplifying

A fraction represents equal parts of a whole. The denominator (bottom) tells you how many equal parts make the whole; the numerator (top) tells you how many parts are counted. In 3/4, the whole is split into four equal parts and three are selected.

Equivalent fractions have the same value. Multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number. To simplify, divide both by their highest common factor.

Worked example: simplify 18/24.
  1. The highest common factor of 18 and 24 is 6.
  2. 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4.
  3. So 18/24 = 3/4.

To compare fractions, use a common denominator. For 2/3 and 3/5, use fifteenths: 2/3 = 10/15 and 3/5 = 9/15, so 2/3 is larger.

6. Decimals and rounding

Digits after the decimal point represent tenths, hundredths, thousandths and so on. To round, locate the digit in the place requested, then look one place to its right. If that next digit is 5–9, increase the target digit by one; if it is 0–4, leave it.

Worked example: round 6.748 to 2 decimal places.

The hundredths digit is 4; the next digit is 8, so round 4 up to 5. The answer is 6.75.

Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit, unlike decimal places, which count places after the decimal point.

7. Percentages of amounts

Percent means “out of 100”, so 35% = 35/100 = 0.35. Find a percentage of an amount by converting it to a decimal and multiplying, or by using known facts such as 50% = half, 25% = a quarter and 10% = divide by ten.

Worked example: find 20% of £45.

10% of £45 is £4.50. Since 20% is twice 10%, double £4.50 to get £9.

For a percentage increase, add the percentage amount; for a decrease, subtract it. Multipliers are quicker: increase by 15% means ×1.15; decrease by 15% means ×0.85.

8. Algebra: notation, substitution and simplifying

Letters represent numbers. In 4x, 4 is multiplied by x. Like terms can be combined: 3x + 2x = 5x, but 3x + 2y cannot be combined because the letters may represent different values.

Worked example: simplify 5a + 3 − 2a + 7.

Combine a terms: 5a − 2a = 3a. Combine constants: 3 + 7 = 10. Answer: 3a + 10.

Substitution means replacing a letter with a number. If x = 4, then 2x + 3 = 2 × 4 + 3 = 11. Use brackets for negative substitutions: if x = −2, then x² = (−2)² = 4.

9. Angles and geometry facts

Acute angles are under 90°, right angles are 90°, obtuse angles are between 90° and 180°, and a straight angle is 180°. Angles on a straight line total 180°; around a point they total 360°. Vertically opposite angles are equal.

Interior angles in a triangle total 180°; in a quadrilateral they total 360°. Parallel lines create equal corresponding and alternate angles, while co-interior angles on the same side of a transversal total 180°.

Worked example: a triangle has angles 48° and 67°.

Known total = 48° + 67° = 115°. Missing angle = 180° − 115° = 65°.

10. Perimeter, area and units

Perimeter is the total distance around a shape, measured in units such as cm. Area is the surface inside it, measured in square units such as cm².

Rectangle area = length × width
Rectangle perimeter = 2(length + width)
Triangle area = ½ × base × perpendicular height

Worked example: rectangle 8 cm by 3 cm.

Area = 8 × 3 = 24 cm². Perimeter = 2(8 + 3) = 22 cm.

Area needs squared units; perimeter does not. Triangle height must be perpendicular to the base.

🐱 Year 8 Maths

Connect number skills to algebra, proportional reasoning and graphs.

1. Fraction operations

For addition and subtraction, first make denominators equal. Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same value to create equivalent fractions. For multiplication, multiply tops together and bottoms together. For division, multiply by the reciprocal (flip) of the second fraction.

Worked example: 1/3 + 1/4

Common denominator 12: 1/3 = 4/12 and 1/4 = 3/12. Add numerators: 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12.

Division example: 3/4 ÷ 2/5

Keep 3/4, change ÷ to ×, and flip 2/5 to 5/2: 3/4 × 5/2 = 15/8 = 1 7/8.

Never add denominators directly. Only add or subtract numerators once the denominators match.
2. Percentage change and multipliers

Percentage change = change ÷ original amount × 100. The original amount belongs in the denominator. For successive changes, apply each multiplier in turn because the second change uses the new value.

Worked example: price rises from £40 to £50.

Change = £10. Percentage change = 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = 25% increase.

A 10% increase is ×1.10; a 10% decrease is ×0.90. Two 10% increases make ×1.1 × 1.1 = 1.21, or 21% overall.

3. Ratio and sharing

A ratio compares quantities. In 2:3 there are 2 parts for the first quantity and 3 parts for the second. Simplify ratios by dividing every part by the same common factor. Convert quantities into the same units before comparing.

Worked example: share £35 in the ratio 2:5.
  1. Total parts = 2 + 5 = 7.
  2. One part = £35 ÷ 7 = £5.
  3. Shares are 2 × £5 = £10 and 5 × £5 = £25.

Check that the shares add to the original total. For a ratio with three parts, add all three parts before finding one part's value.

4. Expanding and factorising expressions

To expand a bracket, multiply the term outside by every term inside. Factorising reverses the process by taking out a common factor. Always multiply every term, including negative terms.

Expand 3(x + 4).

3 × x = 3x and 3 × 4 = 12, so the answer is 3x + 12.

Factorise 6x + 12.

The highest common factor is 6, so the answer is 6(x + 2).

For a negative outside, −2(x − 5) = −2x + 10. A negative multiplied by a negative is positive.

5. Solving linear equations

An equation says both sides have the same value. Keep it balanced by doing the same operation to both sides. Undo operations in reverse order until the letter is isolated.

Solve 3x + 5 = 20.
  1. Subtract 5 from both sides: 3x = 15.
  2. Divide both sides by 3: x = 5.
  3. Check: 3 × 5 + 5 = 20.

When x appears on both sides, collect x terms on one side and constants on the other. Expand brackets first if that makes the equation simpler.

6. Coordinates and straight-line graphs

Coordinates are written (x,y): move horizontally, then vertically. For a graph y = 2x + 1, choose x values, calculate y, plot the coordinate pairs and draw a straight line.

For y = 2x + 1:

x = 0 gives y = 1, so (0,1). x = 1 gives y = 3, so (1,3). x = 2 gives y = 5, so (2,5).

In y = mx + c, m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept. A positive gradient rises from left to right; a negative gradient falls.

7. Probability and sample spaces

Probability runs from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). For equally likely outcomes, probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. A sample space lists every possible outcome, and all outcome probabilities add to 1.

Worked example: probability of an even number on a fair die.

Even results are 2, 4 and 6: three favourable results out of six. Probability = 3/6 = 1/2.

Tables and tree diagrams help list combined outcomes without missing any. If events are independent, the first result does not change the second event's probabilities.

8. Transformations

A reflection flips a shape across a mirror line; a rotation turns it around a centre by an angle and direction; a translation slides every point the same amount. An enlargement changes size using a scale factor and centre.

Translate point (2,1) by vector (−4,3).

Add vector components: x = 2 − 4 = −2, y = 1 + 3 = 4. New point: (−2,4).

For reflection, a point and its image are the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line. A rotation must state centre, angle and direction. For an enlargement, lengths scale by k while angles stay the same.

🦊 Year 9 Maths

Develop algebra, proportion, geometry and statistics skills.

1. Standard form and indices

Standard form writes a number as A × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ A < 10. Move the decimal point until A is between 1 and 10. Moving left gives a positive power for large numbers; moving right gives a negative power for small numbers.

45,000 = 4.5 × 10⁴. For 0.0032, move the decimal three places right to get 3.2, so 3.2 × 10⁻³.

When multiplying powers with the same base, add indices; when dividing, subtract them; when raising a power to a power, multiply the indices. For example, 10² × 10³ = 10⁵ and (10²)³ = 10⁶.

2. Equations with brackets and unknowns on both sides

Expand brackets, collect like terms, then gather x terms on one side and constants on the other. Keep the equation balanced by applying the same operation to both sides.

Solve 4(x − 2) = 2x + 6.
  1. Expand: 4x − 8 = 2x + 6.
  2. Subtract 2x: 2x − 8 = 6.
  3. Add 8: 2x = 14.
  4. Divide by 2: x = 7.

If all x terms cancel and a true statement remains, every value may work. If a false statement remains, no value works.

3. Sequences and the nth term

A sequence follows a rule. In a linear sequence, the same amount is added each time; this is the common difference. Its nth term has form an + b, where a is the common difference.

Find the nth term of 5, 8, 11, 14…

The difference is 3, so start with 3n. This gives 3, 6, 9, 12, which are each 2 below the sequence. Add 2: 3n + 2.

Check the rule by substituting n = 1, 2 and 3. It should reproduce the first three sequence terms exactly.

4. Direct proportion, rates and speed

Direct proportion means one quantity is a constant multiple of another: y = kx. If x doubles, y doubles. Rates compare quantities with different units, such as kilometres per hour or pounds per kilogram.

Five notebooks cost £7.50. One costs £7.50 ÷ 5 = £1.50. Eight cost 8 × £1.50 = £12.

Speed = distance ÷ time
Distance = speed × time
Time = distance ÷ speed

Use consistent units. Convert minutes to hours when speed is in km/h, for example.

5. Pythagoras' theorem

Pythagoras works only in right-angled triangles. The hypotenuse is opposite the 90° angle and is the longest side. If shorter sides are a and b and the hypotenuse is c, then a² + b² = c².

Shorter sides are 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

c² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100. Square-root both sides: c = √100 = 10 cm.

To find a shorter side, subtract the other short side's square from the hypotenuse's square, then square-root: a = √(c² − b²). Identify the hypotenuse before substituting.

6. Circles: circumference and area

The radius r runs from centre to edge. Diameter d goes across the circle through its centre, so d = 2r.

Circumference = 2πr = πd
Area = πr²

Radius 4 cm: circumference = 2π × 4 = 8π ≈ 25.1 cm. Area = π × 4² = 16π ≈ 50.3 cm².

Keep answers in terms of π if asked. Circumference uses length units; area uses squared units. Do not use diameter as the radius.

7. Averages and range

Mean = total ÷ number of values. Median is the middle after sorting. Mode is the most frequent value. Range = largest − smallest.

For 2, 4, 4, 7, 8: total 25, so mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5; median = 4; mode = 4; range = 8 − 2 = 6.

The mean uses every value but is affected by extreme values. The median is often more representative when there is an outlier. Always sort before finding the median.

8. Combined probability

Tree diagrams show sequences of events. Multiply probabilities along one branch to find the probability of that sequence. Add probabilities of separate branches when either branch would satisfy the event.

A fair coin is flipped twice. Two heads: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Exactly one head means HT or TH, so 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2.

If an object is not replaced, probabilities may change after the first selection. If events are independent, the first result does not affect the next probability.

🐼 Year 10 Maths

Apply GCSE methods to algebra, graphs, proportion and geometry.

1. Rearranging formulae

Rearranging means making a chosen letter the subject. Treat the formula as an equation and undo operations in reverse order, doing the same to both sides.

Make x the subject of y = 3x + 5.

Subtract 5: y − 5 = 3x. Divide by 3: x = (y − 5)/3.

If the subject appears in multiple terms, collect those terms first. For example, y = ax + bx = x(a + b), so x = y/(a + b), provided a + b ≠ 0.

2. Simultaneous equations

Simultaneous equations must both be true. The answer is a pair of values satisfying both equations. Elimination adds or subtracts equations to remove one variable; substitution replaces a variable using one equation.

Solve x + y = 10 and x − y = 2.
  1. Add equations: 2x = 12, so x = 6.
  2. Substitute into x + y = 10: 6 + y = 10.
  3. So y = 4. Answer: x = 6, y = 4.

If coefficients do not match, multiply an equation (or both) so one variable has equal coefficients, then add or subtract.

3. Quadratic expressions and factorising

A quadratic has an x² term as its highest power. Factorising rewrites it as a product of brackets. For x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

Factorise x² + 5x + 6.

2 × 3 = 6 and 2 + 3 = 5, so x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

To check, expand the brackets: multiply x by x, calculate the cross terms, then multiply the constants. If the original expression returns, the factorisation is correct.

4. Straight-line graphs and gradient

In y = mx + c, m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept. Gradient measures vertical change divided by horizontal change between two points.

Gradient = (change in y) ÷ (change in x)

Through (1,3) and (4,9): change in y = 6; change in x = 3; gradient = 6 ÷ 3 = 2.

A positive gradient rises from left to right, a negative gradient falls, and a horizontal line has gradient zero. A vertical line's gradient is undefined.

5. Similar shapes and scale factors

Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and proportional matching sides. If length scale factor is k, lengths multiply by k, areas by k² and volumes by k³.

A side of 4 cm corresponds to 10 cm, so scale factor = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5. A matching 6 cm side becomes 6 × 2.5 = 15 cm.

Identify corresponding sides before calculating. If going from the larger shape to the smaller one, divide by the scale factor instead of multiplying.

6. Right-angled trigonometry

Trigonometry links an acute angle in a right triangle to side lengths. Relative to the chosen angle, identify opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle.

SOH: sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse
CAH: cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse
TOA: tan θ = opposite/adjacent

Angle 30°, hypotenuse 10 cm; find opposite. Use sine: sin 30° = opposite/10. Therefore opposite = 10 sin 30° = 10 × 0.5 = 5 cm.

For a missing angle, use inverse trig on the calculator, e.g. θ = sin⁻¹(opposite/hypotenuse). Check the calculator is in degrees when the question uses degrees.

7. Vectors

A vector has magnitude and direction. In a column vector (a,b), a is horizontal movement and b is vertical movement. Add vectors component by component; reverse a vector by changing both signs.

AB = (2,5), BC = (3,−1). To travel A to C via B, AC = AB + BC = (2+3, 5−1) = (5,4).

Parallel vectors are scalar multiples of each other. In a proof, express each route as a vector sum and show that one vector is a multiple of another where needed.

8. Cumulative frequency and box plots

Cumulative frequency is a running total of frequencies, showing how many values are at or below each class boundary. A cumulative frequency graph plots this running total against the upper class boundary.

A box plot summarises minimum, lower quartile Q1, median, upper quartile Q3 and maximum. Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle 50%.

If Q1 = 12, median = 18 and Q3 = 25, IQR = 25 − 12 = 13.

When comparing box plots, compare medians for typical values and IQRs for spread. Include context and units in your conclusion.

🦉 Year 11 Maths

Develop higher-level GCSE skills and explain your reasoning clearly.

1. Solving quadratic equations

A quadratic equation contains an x² term. Solving means finding values of x that make the equation true. Methods include factorising, completing the square and the quadratic formula.

Solve x² + 5x + 6 = 0.

Factorise: (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0. If a product is zero, at least one factor is zero. So x + 2 = 0 gives x = −2, or x + 3 = 0 gives x = −3.

For ax² + bx + c = 0:
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a)

The discriminant b² − 4ac tells the number of real roots: positive gives two, zero gives one repeated root, and negative gives no real roots.

2. Completing the square

Completing the square rewrites a quadratic in a squared-bracket form. This can help solve equations and identify the turning point of a quadratic graph.

Complete the square: x² + 6x + 5.
  1. Half the x coefficient: 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
  2. (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9.
  3. The original constant is 4 less than 9.

So x² + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)² − 4.

For x² + bx + c, use (x + b/2)² + c − (b/2)². If the x² coefficient is not 1, factor it out of the x² and x terms first.

3. Iteration and approximations

Iteration applies a formula repeatedly, using each output as the next input. It can approximate a solution that is difficult to find exactly. Use the stated starting value and keep sufficient calculator digits between steps.

Given xₙ₊₁ = (xₙ + 5)/2 and x₁ = 3: x₂ = (3 + 5)/2 = 4; x₃ = (4 + 5)/2 = 4.5.

Continue until the required number of iterations or accuracy is reached. Round only as instructed, usually at the end, to avoid accumulating rounding error.

4. Functions and composite functions

A function maps an input to an output. f(x) means the output of function f when the input is x. If f(x) = 2x + 3, then f(4) = 2 × 4 + 3 = 11.

A composite function applies one function after another. f(g(x)) means calculate g first, then put that result into f. Order matters.

f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x². Find f(g(3)): g(3) = 9, then f(9) = 2 × 9 + 1 = 19.

To find an inverse, write y = f(x), swap x and y, then rearrange to make y the subject. An inverse reverses the original mapping.

5. Circle theorems

Circle theorems describe angle relationships. Learn the condition for each theorem and mark equal angles on your diagram as soon as you spot it.

  • The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference subtended by the same arc.
  • Angles in the same segment are equal.
  • The angle in a semicircle is 90°.
  • Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°.
  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
  • Tangents from the same external point are equal in length.

An angle at the circumference is 35°. The angle at the centre on the same arc is 2 × 35° = 70°.

In exam solutions, name the theorem you used. This explains why the angle relationship is valid rather than simply stating a number.

6. Vector proofs

Vector proofs show a relationship using directed journeys. To travel A to C via B, add AB and BC. Reversing a journey changes the signs. A point dividing a line in a ratio can be expressed as a fraction of the whole vector.

If AB = a and BC = 2b, then AC = a + 2b. If D is the midpoint of AC, then AD = ½(a + 2b) = ½a + b.

Parallel vectors are scalar multiples. For a proof, express routes clearly, simplify, and show that the relevant vectors are multiples of each other when you need to establish parallel lines.

7. Conditional probability and Venn diagrams

A Venn diagram shows sets and overlaps. A ∩ B means in both sets; A ∪ B means in A or B or both. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together.

Conditional probability asks for P(A given B). Once B is known to have happened, only outcomes in B remain possible.

P(A given B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B), when P(B) ≠ 0

Of 30 students, 12 study French and 8 study both French and Spanish. Given that a student studies French, probability they also study Spanish = 8/12 = 2/3.

Use the condition to choose the denominator. A frequent mistake is dividing by the full group when the question has restricted the group.

8. Bounds and error intervals

Rounded measurements represent a range of possible original values. If a length is 7 cm to the nearest centimetre, it could be from 6.5 cm up to, but not including, 7.5 cm.

For rounding to a given unit, the bounds are half a unit below and half a unit above the rounded value. The lower bound is included; the upper bound is usually excluded because it rounds to the next value.

240 g to the nearest 10 g means 235 ≤ mass < 245, because half of 10 is 5.

For positive multiplication, minimum values usually use lower bounds and maximum values use upper bounds. For positive division, a minimum usually uses the smallest numerator and largest denominator.

9. Histograms and frequency density

Histograms display grouped continuous data, including classes of different widths. Each bar's area represents frequency, so the height is frequency density, not necessarily frequency.

Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width

A class interval has width 10 and frequency 30. Frequency density = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.

Put class boundaries on the horizontal axis and frequency density on the vertical axis. The bar areas should be proportional to their frequencies.

10. Exam problem-solving strategy

Read the question carefully and identify exactly what it asks for. Underline units and relevant information. Choose a method or formula, show the steps, and check whether the answer is sensible.

  1. Read: identify the target and units.
  2. Plan: choose a rule, formula, diagram or algebraic method.
  3. Show: write clear working so the reasoning can be followed.
  4. Check: estimate, check units and substitute solutions back when possible.
  5. Review: if stuck, write down what is known and make a useful first step.

A circle has diameter 8 cm. Do not use 8 as the radius. First r = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 cm, then area = π × 4² = 16π cm².

Avoid rounding too early. Clear method and working can earn marks even if a later arithmetic step goes wrong.
