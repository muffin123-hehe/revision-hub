1. The Norman Conquest Year 7 · 1066 · Kings and power ⌄

Why was 1066 important?

In 1066, King Edward the Confessor died without a direct heir. Several people claimed the English throne. Harold Godwinson became king, but William, Duke of Normandy, believed he had a right to rule England. Harald Hardrada of Norway also invaded.

Harold defeated Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge in September 1066. He then marched south to face William's army at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October. William's army won, and Harold was killed. William was crowned king on Christmas Day 1066.

How did William control England?

William rewarded supporters with land and built castles to strengthen Norman control. Motte-and-bailey castles could be built relatively quickly. He also replaced many English nobles with Norman lords. Resistance continued in parts of England, so William used harsh punishments, including the Harrying of the North.

Remember: William's victory changed England's ruling class, land ownership, castles and links with Europe.

Key vocabulary

  • Claimant: person claiming a throne
  • Conquest: taking control by force
  • Feudal system: hierarchy of land and loyalty
Revision tip: Practise explaining why William won. Consider leadership, tactics, Harold's earlier battle and the armies' positions.
2. Medieval life and the feudal system Year 7 · Society · Land and loyalty ⌄

How was medieval society organised?

Medieval England was mainly an agricultural society. Most people lived in villages and worked on the land. After the Norman Conquest, land and loyalty were central to the feudal system. The king granted land to powerful nobles, who owed loyalty and military service. Lords granted land or rights to others, while peasants worked the land and owed duties.

Life depended greatly on wealth, status and where a person lived. Peasants could face heavy work and rents. The Church influenced education, festivals, beliefs and everyday life. Towns grew through trade and crafts, although most people remained connected to farming.

Castles and control

Castles were military strongholds, homes for lords and symbols of authority. Stone castles generally offered stronger defences than early wooden structures but required more time and resources to build.

Big idea: Land created wealth and power. Social status affected people's duties and opportunities.

Key vocabulary

  • Noble: member of the upper class
  • Peasant: rural worker
  • Manor: estate controlled by a lord
3. The Black Death Year 7 · 1348–1349 · Disease and society ⌄

What was the Black Death?

The Black Death was a devastating plague pandemic that reached England in 1348. It spread through trade networks and caused enormous loss of life. People at the time did not understand bacteria or how the disease spread, so explanations included religious ideas and theories about polluted air.

What changed?

The population fell sharply. With fewer workers available, surviving labourers could sometimes demand better wages or conditions. Landowners struggled to find enough workers, and some land was used differently. These changes did not happen equally everywhere or immediately.

In 1351, the English government introduced the Statute of Labourers, attempting to limit wages and restrict workers' movement. Such measures show why labour shortages could challenge existing social arrangements.

Cause and consequence: The disease caused population loss; population loss contributed to labour shortages and changes in bargaining power.

Key vocabulary

  • Pandemic: widespread disease outbreak
  • Labour: work performed by people
  • Consequence: result of an event
1. Henry VIII and the Reformation Year 8 · 1509–1547 · Religion and monarchy ⌄

Why did Henry VIII break with Rome?

Henry VIII became king in 1509. He wanted a male heir and sought to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon after they had no surviving son. The Pope did not grant the annulment Henry wanted. Henry's desire to control the English Church, political concerns and the succession crisis all contributed to the break with Rome.

The Reformation Parliament passed laws that reduced the Pope's authority in England. The Act of Supremacy of 1534 recognised Henry as the supreme head of the Church of England. The monasteries were dissolved and their wealth transferred to the Crown and other owners.

Did religion change immediately?

Not entirely. Under Henry, many traditional Catholic beliefs and practices remained, even as royal control of the Church changed. Later monarchs made further religious changes. Edward VI promoted Protestant reforms, Mary I restored Catholicism, and Elizabeth I established a lasting religious settlement in 1559.

Remember: The English Reformation involved religion, political power, money and the royal succession.

Key vocabulary

  • Reformation: major religious change
  • Annulment: declaration that a marriage was invalid
  • Monastery: religious community building
Exam tip: Avoid saying Henry changed everything for only one reason. Explain how personal, political and religious factors connected.
2. Elizabeth I and the Spanish Armada Year 8 · 1558–1603 · Conflict and monarchy ⌄

Why was Elizabeth's reign challenging?

Elizabeth I became queen in 1558. She faced religious divisions, questions about who should succeed her, and tensions with Spain. Spain's Philip II opposed aspects of Elizabeth's religious policy and English support for attacks on Spanish interests.

In 1588, the Spanish Armada sailed to help facilitate an invasion of England. English ships, the use of fireships at Calais, Spanish difficulties and severe weather contributed to the Armada's failure to achieve its objective. It was not simply a story of English ships defeating Spain in one battle.

Why does the Armada matter?

Its failure strengthened Elizabeth's position and became an important part of the story of her reign. However, Spain remained a major power, and conflict between the two countries continued after 1588.

Key date: 1588 — the Spanish Armada campaign.

Key vocabulary

  • Armada: large fleet of ships
  • Invasion: entering a country by force
  • Propaganda: material designed to influence opinions
3. The English Civil War Year 8 · 1642–1651 · King versus Parliament ⌄

Why did civil war begin?

Tensions grew between King Charles I and Parliament over taxation, royal authority and religion. Charles's attempts to govern without Parliament and disagreements about the Church contributed to a wider political crisis. Fighting began in 1642 between Royalists, who supported the king, and Parliamentarians, who fought for Parliament.

Parliament's forces eventually won. Charles I was tried and executed in 1649. England became a republic, known as the Commonwealth, and later Oliver Cromwell became Lord Protector. The monarchy was restored under Charles II in 1660.

Big idea: The war challenged the relationship between the monarch and Parliament and raised questions about where political authority should lie.

Key vocabulary

  • Royalist: supporter of the king
  • Parliamentarian: supporter of Parliament
  • Republic: state without a monarch as head of state
1. The Industrial Revolution Year 9 · c.1750 onwards · Factories and change ⌄

What was the Industrial Revolution?

From the eighteenth century, production in Britain changed as machinery, factories and new sources of power transformed many industries. Textile production grew, coal and iron became increasingly important, and steam engines helped power factories, mines and transport.

Britain's industrial growth had several causes, including access to coal, investment, trade, inventions, labour and transport networks. The process was gradual and varied between regions and industries.

How did life change?

Many people moved from the countryside to growing industrial towns and cities. Factory work could involve long hours, dangerous machinery and low pay. Overcrowded housing and poor sanitation contributed to disease. Industrialisation also created jobs, increased production and helped expand railways and trade.

Campaigners and reformers pushed for changes to working conditions. Factory legislation gradually introduced restrictions and protections, although enforcement and improvements took time.

Balanced answer: Industrialisation created wealth and new technology, but many workers faced difficult conditions.

Key vocabulary

  • Urbanisation: growth of towns and cities
  • Industrialisation: growth of machine-based industry
  • Reform: change intended to improve something
2. The British Empire Year 9 · Expansion, trade and resistance ⌄

How did the British Empire grow?

The British Empire developed over centuries through trade, settlement, warfare, conquest and political agreements. Its size and form changed over time. Britain's navy and commercial networks helped it expand, while companies and colonial governments exercised power in different places.

What were the effects?

The empire connected territories through trade and migration, but imperial rule was unequal. In many places, land and resources were taken or controlled by colonial authorities. Enslavement, forced labour, racial discrimination and violence shaped parts of imperial history. People under colonial rule also resisted through rebellions, political organising and independence movements.

Britain abolished its transatlantic slave trade in 1807, and slavery was abolished across most of the British Empire by legislation passed in 1833, with the main provisions taking effect in 1834. These changes followed long campaigns by enslaved people, abolitionists and other activists.

When revising: Examine who benefited, who suffered, how people resisted, and how experiences differed across regions and time periods.

Key vocabulary

  • Empire: territories ruled by a central power
  • Colonialism: control over another territory
  • Abolition: ending a law or practice
3. The causes of the First World War Year 9 · 1914 · Alliances and tension ⌄

Why did war break out in 1914?

Europe experienced growing tensions before 1914. Militarism, alliances, imperial rivalry and nationalism are often used to organise the main long-term causes. Countries expanded armed forces, competed for influence and formed agreements that affected how they responded to international crises.

On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary was assassinated in Sarajevo. The July Crisis followed, as Austria-Hungary, Serbia, Russia, Germany, France and Britain made decisions that widened the conflict. Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914 after Germany invaded Belgium.

Long-term and short-term causes

The assassination was the immediate trigger, but it did not explain the whole war. Earlier rivalries, military planning, political decisions and the alliance system help explain why a regional crisis became a much larger war.

Remember: Distinguish between long-term causes and the immediate trigger of war.

Key vocabulary

  • Alliance: agreement between countries
  • Militarism: emphasis on military power
  • Nationalism: strong identification with a nation
1. The First World War Year 10 · 1914–1918 · Warfare and society ⌄

What was trench warfare?

On the Western Front, armies dug extensive trench systems. Barbed wire, machine guns, artillery and difficult terrain made attacks costly. Soldiers faced mud, disease, poor sanitation, shelling and the psychological strain of combat. Conditions varied by location, season and military role.

Why was the war so destructive?

Industrial production supplied weapons and ammunition on a scale not seen in many earlier wars. Artillery caused many casualties, and new technologies such as tanks, aircraft and chemical weapons affected how fighting developed. The war also relied on resources, labour and finance from societies far behind the front lines.

How did the war affect Britain?

The government expanded its role in the economy and society. Women took on a wider range of jobs as many men served in the armed forces. Their contribution was important, but women's experiences differed and the changes did not automatically produce equality in every area.

The armistice with Germany took effect on 11 November 1918. The Treaty of Versailles, signed in 1919, set peace terms with Germany and included territorial losses, military restrictions and reparations.

Useful distinction: An armistice stops the fighting; a peace treaty establishes formal peace terms.

Key vocabulary

  • Armistice: agreement to stop fighting
  • Conscription: compulsory military service
  • Home Front: civilian life during wartime
Exam tip: Use precise examples when explaining the impact of warfare on soldiers and civilians.
2. The Second World War Year 10 · 1939–1945 · Global conflict ⌄

Why did the Second World War begin?

The peace settlement after the First World War, the Great Depression, the growth of aggressive dictatorships and the failure of international efforts to prevent expansion all contributed to the crisis. Nazi Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September.

Britain in the war

In 1940, Britain fought Germany during the Battle of Britain, an air campaign linked to Germany's plans to gain air superiority. The Blitz involved sustained bombing of British towns and cities. Civilians experienced evacuation, rationing, air raids and disruption to everyday life.

The war became truly global, involving European, African, Asian and Pacific theatres. Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941. Japan attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941, bringing the United States directly into the war. The Allies eventually defeated Germany in May 1945; Japan surrendered later that year.

What was the Holocaust?

The Holocaust was the systematic persecution and murder of six million Jewish people by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. Nazi persecution also targeted Roma people, disabled people, Polish and Soviet civilians, Soviet prisoners of war, political opponents, gay men and other groups. Their experiences were not identical, and the Holocaust must be studied with care and respect.

Key dates: 1939 — war begins in Europe; 1940 — Battle of Britain; 1944 — D-Day; 1945 — war in Europe ends and Japan surrenders later that year.

Key vocabulary

  • Evacuation: moving people to safety
  • Rationing: controlling access to scarce goods
  • Genocide: intent to destroy a protected group
3. Nazi Germany and dictatorship Year 10 · 1933–1945 · Power and persecution ⌄

How did Hitler establish a dictatorship?

The Nazi Party gained support during a period of political instability and economic hardship. Hitler became Chancellor of Germany in January 1933. After the Reichstag Fire, emergency measures were used against political opponents. The Enabling Act of March 1933 allowed Hitler's government to make laws without normal parliamentary approval, helping dismantle democracy.

The Nazi regime used propaganda, censorship, intimidation, secret police and concentration camps to control society. Opposition could be dangerous, but some individuals and groups resisted. The regime also promoted racist ideas and expanded persecution, which escalated into genocide during the war.

How should you explain dictatorship?

Consider how a leader gains power, how laws and institutions change, how force and propaganda are used, and how people respond. Separate the reasons people supported a regime from the methods it used to maintain control.

Key idea: Dictatorships can use a combination of legal changes, coercion, propaganda and the destruction of political opposition.

Key vocabulary

  • Dictatorship: government with concentrated power
  • Censorship: controlling what can be published
  • Propaganda: information designed to influence people
1. GCSE History: source analysis Year 11 · Skills practice · Sources and evidence ⌄

How do you analyse a historical source?

A historical source is evidence from or about the past. Sources include letters, photographs, speeches, newspapers, diaries, official records and objects. Before using a source, identify what it says or shows and connect that information to the historical context.

Consider who created the source, when it was produced, its purpose and its intended audience. These details can help explain why the source presents events in a particular way. However, a source should not be dismissed simply because its creator had a viewpoint. Ask what it can reliably tell you about the specific question.

A useful answer structure

  1. Make a clear point about what the source suggests.
  2. Use a short, precise quotation or visual detail.
  3. Explain what that detail reveals.
  4. Connect it to accurate contextual knowledge.
  5. Address the question directly.
Remember: Provenance means information about a source's origin, such as its author, date and purpose. Provenance is useful when it helps answer the question.

Key vocabulary

  • Provenance: origin of a source
  • Inference: conclusion drawn from evidence
  • Context: relevant historical circumstances
  • Reliability: how far evidence can be trusted for a purpose
2. GCSE History: explaining causes and consequences Year 11 · Structured paragraphs · Causation ⌄

How do you write a strong explanation?

A strong history explanation does more than list facts. It answers the question, selects relevant evidence and explains why that evidence matters. For a question about causes, identify factors and explain how they contributed to the event. For consequences, show what changed and why.

Try this paragraph structure

  1. Point: state your main reason or argument.
  2. Evidence: provide an accurate date, event or detail.
  3. Explain: show how the evidence supports your point.
  4. Link: connect the explanation to the question.

For example, when explaining why the Norman Conquest mattered, do not just state that William built castles. Explain that castles helped establish military control and demonstrate Norman authority in England.

Important: The exact question formats and mark schemes depend on your GCSE exam board and specification. Practise using the format your teacher gives you.

Useful vocabulary

  • Factor: a contributing reason
  • Significance: importance of an event
  • Turning point: event that changes the direction of developments
  • Interpretation: an explanation of the past
3. GCSE History: revision and exam planning Year 11 · Revision methods · Exam readiness ⌄

How should you revise history?

Start by checking your exam board specification and the topics your class has studied. Make a checklist of the knowledge and skills you need. Use active recall by answering questions without looking at your notes, then check your answers and correct gaps.

Practise timelines, key people, dates, causes, consequences and changes over time. Compare similar events to avoid mixing them up. Complete timed exam questions and use teacher feedback or your exam board's mark scheme to identify specific improvements.

A simple 30-minute revision session

  1. 5 minutes: write down everything you remember.
  2. 10 minutes: check notes and correct missing information.
  3. 10 minutes: answer one exam-style question.
  4. 5 minutes: mark your answer and record what to revisit.
GCSE reminder: There is no single universal GCSE History course. Content and question types differ between exam boards, so revise the specification you actually study.

Before your exam

  • Check the question wording carefully.
  • Use specific evidence, not vague statements.
  • Explain your reasoning instead of just listing facts.
  • Manage time across all questions.
  • Leave a moment to check that each answer addresses the question.
